Executives at Halloway & Crane, one of the country's largest advertising agencies, admitted Tuesday during an industry panel that the firm deliberately designs commercials to be as annoying as possible, explaining that "irritation is the most reliable path to brand recall known to science."

"People think we're trying to make ads they'll enjoy," said Halloway & Crane Chief Creative Officer Derek Stanton. "That's adorable. Enjoyment fades. Irritation lingers. If you like an ad, you forget it by dinner. If you hate an ad, you'll be thinking about it in the shower three weeks later. And while you're thinking about it, you're thinking about us."

Stanton said the agency's research team has spent a decade refining what it calls the Annoyance Retention Model, a proprietary system that measures how much an ad irritates viewers and how long that irritation keeps the brand in their minds.

"The sweet spot is about a 7.8 out of 10 on our irritation scale," Stanton said. "Below 7, people ignore it. Above 9, they throw the remote. But at 7.8, they sigh deeply, roll their eyes, and remember our client's phone number for the rest of their lives."

The agency's most successful campaign, for auto insurer Shieldway Mutual, features a man in a bright orange suit singing a four-note jingle, "Shield-way, Shield-waaay," which is repeated 11 times in a 30-second spot. The ad scored a 7.9 on the irritation scale and increased Shieldway's brand awareness by 340%.

"Nobody likes the orange man," Stanton said. "Our focus groups describe him as 'unbearable,' 'haunting,' and 'why is he like this.' But 94% of Americans can now sing the jingle. Three percent of them sing it involuntarily while falling asleep."

When asked whether deliberately annoying viewers might harm the brand, Stanton shook his head.

"Hatred is just memorability with feelings," he said. "The feelings fade. The memory stays. When those viewers need car insurance, the first name that pops into their head will be the one they hate. And they'll call. Out of exhaustion."

Other agencies on the panel quietly nodded. "Everyone does it," one executive admitted. "We just don't say it out loud."

Psychologists say the strategy exploits a real phenomenon known as the "mere exposure effect," in which repeated exposure to something increases familiarity, even when the exposure is unpleasant. "These companies have found a way to turn annoyance into a mental tattoo," said Dr. Rebecca Moore, a cognitive psychologist. "It's not persuasion. It's erosion."

Viewers say the confession explains a lot.

"I always thought, who approved this?" said Tampa resident Chris Alvarez, 42. "Now I know. Someone approved it on purpose. They sat in a room and said, 'Let's make this man sing the same four notes until people want to move to the woods.'"

At press time, Halloway & Crane had announced its next campaign, which will feature the orange man singing the Shieldway jingle backward, a variation the agency projects will score an 8.2.

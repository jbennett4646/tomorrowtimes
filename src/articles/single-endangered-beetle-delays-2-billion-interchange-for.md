A $2 billion highway interchange project in central California has resumed construction after a six-year delay caused by the discovery of a single specimen of the endangered Valley Copper Beetle near the project site, a delay environmental officials now concede may have been unnecessary after the beetle was discovered thriving in a Walmart parking lot three miles away.

"We take environmental protection very seriously," said state transportation spokesperson Daniel Ruiz. "When a biologist found a Valley Copper Beetle in a patch of grass near the proposed interchange, we immediately halted all work. It was the responsible thing to do."

The beetle, a small iridescent insect believed to number fewer than 2,000 in the wild, triggered a federal habitat assessment, a multi-agency consultation, a 400-page mitigation plan, two lawsuits from advocacy groups, and the construction of a $14 million "beetle corridor," a fenced strip of native grassland designed to allow the insects to safely cross beneath the highway.

Six years later, a graduate student studying invasive weeds discovered a thriving colony of more than 600 Valley Copper Beetles living in a landscaped median of a Walmart parking lot.

"They seemed very happy," said the student, Emily Tran. "They were crawling all over the shrubs near the cart corral. I counted at least 40 near the Redbox kiosk. They seemed completely unbothered by traffic, shoppers, or the guy selling phone cases at the entrance."

Further surveys revealed beetle populations living near a Taco Bell, a Jiffy Lube, and the landscaping outside a regional DMV office.

"They're actually doing better in commercial areas," Tran said. "There's mulch, irrigation, and no predators. I think the beetle prefers strip malls."

The $14 million beetle corridor, completed in 2025, has not been used by a single beetle. Wildlife cameras installed along the corridor have recorded three rabbits, one coyote, and a teenager on a scooter.

"We built them a beautiful, dedicated pathway," Ruiz said. "They chose the Walmart. Honestly, it's hard to blame them. It has better parking."

Environmental advocates defended the delay, noting that protecting endangered species requires caution. "We didn't know at the time that the beetle was so adaptable," said one advocate. "Now we know. And that's science."

At press time, a new endangered species, a rare desert snail, had been discovered near the interchange, and the project was paused again while officials checked the Walmart.

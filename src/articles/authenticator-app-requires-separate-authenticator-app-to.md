Security researchers confirmed Thursday that the popular authenticator app SecureKey now requires users to verify their identity through a separate authenticator app before it will open, creating what experts are calling "the first fully recursive login experience."

"It's security all the way down," said Dr. Priya Raman, a cybersecurity professor at a local university. "You open the authenticator to get a code for your bank. But the authenticator wants you to prove you're you. So it sends a code to a second authenticator. And the second authenticator wants a code from a third authenticator. Eventually, you're just holding your phone, staring into the abyss, and the abyss is asking for a six-digit number."

SecureKey announced the change in a blog post Monday, describing it as "a major step forward in protecting our users from unauthorized access to their authentication."

"Authenticator apps are only as secure as the device they're on," the post read. "If someone gains access to your phone, they could gain access to your codes. That's why we've added a new layer of protection: authentication for your authentication. Now, before you can access your codes, you'll need a code."

The required second app, SecureKey Guardian, is available free on major app stores. It requires its own setup, including a password, a recovery phrase, facial recognition, and, according to user reports, "a brief conversation about your values."

Users say the process has made logging in nearly impossible.

"I just wanted to check my email," said Portland resident Eric Dawson, 38. "My email asked for a code from SecureKey. SecureKey asked for a code from Guardian. Guardian asked me to confirm my identity through my phone's face scanner. My phone didn't recognize me because I'd just woken up and I looked like a raccoon. So Guardian locked me out. Now I can't get into SecureKey, which means I can't get into my email, which is where the recovery link for Guardian gets sent."

Dawson said he has been locked out of his entire digital life for three days. "I'm just existing now," he said. "I've read two books. I went for a walk. I talked to a neighbor. It's terrifying."

SecureKey acknowledged the issue and said it is developing a solution: a third app, SecureKey Guardian Recovery, which will allow users who are locked out of Guardian to regain access by verifying their identity with a code from SecureKey.

"That should close the loop," a SecureKey spokesperson said.

When reminded that SecureKey requires Guardian to open, the spokesperson paused for several seconds before saying, "We'll get back to you on that."

Security experts say the trend is part of a broader escalation. "Every time we add a layer of protection, we add a layer of friction," Raman said. "At some point, the system is so secure that it protects you from yourself. Your accounts are completely safe. You just can't get into any of them. Which, technically, is the most secure configuration possible."

At press time, SecureKey had announced it was acquiring a startup that makes authenticator apps for authenticator apps for authenticator apps, calling the deal "a natural extension of our mission."

Phoenix resident David Moreno, 44, switched his car insurance provider from Homeguard to Sentinel Direct on Tuesday in a desperate attempt to escape Homeguard's relentless advertising campaign, only to be bombarded by Sentinel Direct ads within 11 minutes of signing his new policy.

"I did it for my sanity," Moreno said. "Homeguard was everywhere. Their ads were on TV, on YouTube, on the radio, on Instagram, on podcasts, on billboards, on the gas pump, and once, somehow, on my smart fridge. I figured if I switched, they'd leave me alone. Maybe they'd give up. That was my plan."

Moreno said he finalized his Sentinel Direct policy online and closed his laptop. When he opened his phone to celebrate by scrolling social media, the first post he saw was a Sentinel Direct ad featuring the company's talking armadillo mascot, asking, "Have you considered switching to Sentinel Direct?"

"I already did," Moreno said. "I just did it. Ten minutes ago. The armadillo didn't care. He kept asking."

Over the next 24 hours, Moreno said he received 312 Sentinel Direct ads across seven platforms, including one that played before a YouTube video about how to make ads stop.

"The ad was about switching to Sentinel," he said. "I'm already with Sentinel. I'm paying them money. They're spending my money to convince me to give them the money I'm already giving them."

Advertising experts say Moreno's experience is common. "Once you engage with a brand, you're tagged as a high-intent customer," said digital marketing consultant Jenna Park. "The algorithm doesn't know you already bought. It just knows you were interested. So it shows you more ads. In a sense, becoming a customer makes you a bigger target."

Meanwhile, Homeguard has not stopped advertising to Moreno either. Since canceling his policy, he has received dozens of "We Miss You" ads featuring Homeguard's cheerful saleswoman, Flo-Anne, standing alone in an empty office looking at a framed photo of Moreno's car.

"It's emotionally manipulative," Moreno said. "She looks so sad. There's rain on the window behind her. She whispers, 'Come back, David.' I don't know how she knows my name."

Moreno said he briefly considered switching to a third insurance provider, but realized that would simply add a third company's ads to the rotation.

"It's like a trap," he said. "Every company I switch to just joins the chorus. At this point, the only way to escape insurance ads is to stop driving, sell my car, move to a cabin in the woods, and throw my phone in a lake. And I'm sure there's a billboard in the woods."

At press time, Moreno had received an ad from a fourth insurance company promising "fewer ads when you switch," which he described as "the most insulting thing I've ever seen."

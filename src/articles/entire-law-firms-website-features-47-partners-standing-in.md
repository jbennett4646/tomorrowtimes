Visitors to the website of regional law firm Hargrove, Whitley & Pruitt LLP have begun reporting a strange sensation of unease after viewing the firm's "Meet Our Team" page, which features 47 partners, each standing at the same three-quarter angle, wearing the same confident half-smile, in front of the same softly blurred glass office tower.

"I was looking for an estate attorney," said Charlotte resident Linda Morris, 61. "I clicked on 'Our People' and just kept scrolling. Man in a navy suit, arms crossed, slight smile, office tower behind him. Woman in a navy blazer, arms crossed, slight smile, office tower behind her. Another man. Same tower. Same smile. By the 20th one, I felt like I was trapped in a hall of mirrors. I closed my laptop and sat in the dark for a while."

The headshots were produced last spring by Lumora AI, a startup that generates professional portraits from a handful of selfies uploaded by employees. According to the firm, the switch saved roughly $14,000 in photography costs and eliminated "the inconvenience of having everyone in the same building on the same day."

"We wanted a cohesive, polished look," said managing partner Gregory Whitley, whose own headshot depicts him with a full head of hair he has not possessed since 2009. "Consistency communicates professionalism. When clients see our team, we want them to think, 'This is a firm that has its act together.'"

Clients report thinking other things.

"I couldn't tell them apart," said business owner Derek Hall, who has worked with the firm for six years. "I've met my attorney, Steve, in person at least 30 times. I went to the website to get his email, and I genuinely could not find him. I narrowed it down to four guys. They all looked like Steve. None of them looked like Steve. One of them might have been Steve's wife."

Firm employees say the confusion extends internally.

"Last month, someone from accounting walked up to me in the break room and asked if I was Brian," said associate Karen Liu. "I'm not Brian. Brian is a 58-year-old tax partner. But in our headshots we have the same jawline, the same teeth, the same lighting, and apparently the same soul. She'd been staring at the website for a week trying to memorize faces and gave up."

Photography experts say the effect is a hallmark of AI-generated portraits. "The software produces a version of you that's statistically 'professional,'" said Dr. Rachel Kim, who studies digital imaging. "That means it smooths your skin, straightens your posture, whitens your teeth, and drifts your features toward an average face. Do that to 47 people at once, and the average wins. You end up with a firm of cousins."

The office tower in the background has drawn particular scrutiny. Although the firm's actual office is located in a two-story brick building next to a Chick-fil-A, every headshot shows a glittering glass skyscraper rising behind each partner.

"We don't have a tower," admitted office manager Paula Greer. "We have a parking lot and a drainage ditch. But the AI said the tower 'conveyed stability.' Honestly, I've started to believe the tower is real. Sometimes I look out the window and feel disappointed."

Several partners' spouses have also raised concerns.

"My husband's photo looks like a younger, more successful man I might have married instead of him," said one partner's wife, who asked not to be named. "I'm not saying I'm upset about it. I'm saying I've been looking at it a lot."

Whitley said the firm has no plans to change the headshots, noting that the website's traffic has increased 40% since they were introduced, a figure analysts attribute largely to visitors sending the page to friends with the message "Look at this."

At press time, the firm had hired a new associate, whose AI headshot was generated, uploaded, and approved before anyone noticed he had already been on the website for three years as a different partner.

Spokane resident Kevin Wu, 41, has begun questioning whether he is actually a human being after being asked to verify that he is not a robot for the 11th time in a single day, and failing four of the tests.

"It started with the traffic lights," Wu said, rubbing his eyes. "I clicked all the traffic lights. It said try again. I clicked them again, plus the one that was sort of half in the frame. It said try again. By the third try, I was questioning whether a pole counts as part of the traffic light. I still don't know. Nobody knows."

Over the course of Tuesday, Wu was asked to identify traffic lights, crosswalks, fire hydrants, buses, motorcycles, bicycles, staircases, mountains, chimneys, and, in one case, "all squares containing sadness."

"I got the sadness one wrong," Wu said. "I thought it was the picture of the empty parking lot. Apparently it was the bus stop."

By early evening, Wu said, the repeated tests had begun to take a psychological toll.

"You click 'I am not a robot' and it says, 'Are you sure?' And you start thinking, am I? I get up at the same time every day. I eat the same breakfast. I sit in the same traffic. I go to the same job where I answer the same emails. Maybe the internet knows something I don't."

Wu said the final straw came when he failed to identify all squares containing a bicycle.

"There was a tiny wheel in the corner of one square," he said. "Is that a bicycle? Is part of a bicycle a bicycle? It's a philosophical question. I clicked it. It said wrong. Then it said, 'Verification failed. You may be a robot.' I just sat there staring at my screen, wondering if I was going to start beeping."

Wu has since started seeing a therapist, who has asked him to prove his humanity using a different method.

"She asked me to describe a memory that made me feel something," Wu said. "I told her about my grandfather teaching me to fish. She said, 'That's very human.' I asked her if she could put that in writing so I could send it to the websites."

Technology experts say CAPTCHA tests have become increasingly difficult as artificial intelligence improves. "Modern AI is extremely good at identifying traffic lights," said computer scientist Dr. Lila Moreno. "So the tests keep getting harder to distinguish humans from bots. At this point, the only reliable way to prove you're human is to fail the test in a very specific, frustrated way. Robots don't sigh."

Some websites have begun experimenting with alternative verification methods. One airline now asks users to describe "a time you felt disappointed by an airline." A major retailer asks users to "rate your current level of exhaustion from 1 to 10." Anyone who answers "10" is immediately verified as human.

At press time, Wu had been flagged by his own bank's fraud system as "a sophisticated bot impersonating Kevin Wu," and was asked to visit a branch with two forms of ID and a sworn statement that he has feelings.

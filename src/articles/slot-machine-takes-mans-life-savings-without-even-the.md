Retired machinist Frank Delaney, 71, of Sioux Falls lost his entire life savings Wednesday night to a Strip slot machine that, according to Delaney, "never once made a sound that seemed to acknowledge what it was doing to me."

"In the old days, the machine clunked," Delaney said, staring at the glowing touchscreen of a game called *Dragon Fortune Mega Cascade Infinity.* "You pulled the lever, the reels spun, and when it took your money, it went *clunk.* Like a man shutting a door. There was dignity in that. This thing just made a noise like a xylophone falling down the stairs and showed me a cartoon tiger."

Delaney said he sat down at 8 p.m. intending to spend $20, but quickly lost track of what was happening. The screen, he said, displayed dozens of flashing symbols, multiple progressive meters, a "Bonus Wheel of Destiny," and colors "flashing like a Tokyo subway during a seizure."

"I couldn't tell if I was winning or losing," Delaney said. "Every spin, the machine would celebrate. Fireworks, confetti, a little dragon dancing. I'd look down and I'd have less money. The dragon kept dancing. I think the dragon was laughing at me."

By 3:40 a.m., Delaney had lost $184,000.

Slot manufacturers say modern machines are designed to create an engaging, immersive experience. "Players want excitement," said one game designer, speaking on condition of anonymity. "The old machines were boring. They told you plainly when you lost. That's a terrible user experience. Our machines celebrate every outcome equally, so the player never has to feel the sting of reality."

The designer confirmed that the machines contain no actual slots, no reels, and no lever, though some models include a decorative lever that is "emotionally load-bearing" and connected to nothing.

Casino veterans say the change reflects a broader shift in how Vegas relates to its customers.

"When I started, a slot machine was a machine," said retired floor manager Doris Ambrose, 83. "It had gears and springs. You could hear it thinking. Now it's a tablet that screams at you. You're just slapping glass while your money evaporates. At least the old ones had the courtesy to clunk when they took your soul."

Delaney said he plans to return home and "sit very quietly in a room with no screens for several years."

At press time, the machine had awarded Delaney a free spin, which he was informed was worth zero dollars but included "three seconds of celebratory music."

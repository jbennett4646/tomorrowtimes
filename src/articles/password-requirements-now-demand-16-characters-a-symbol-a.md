Austin resident Melissa Grant, 36, spent 47 minutes Monday attempting to create a new password for her utility company's website, eventually succeeding only after providing 16 characters, three symbols, an original haiku, and a small blood sample pressed against her laptop's webcam.

"It started normal," Grant said, holding a cotton ball against her fingertip. "At least eight characters, one capital letter, one number. Fine. Then it said, 'Password must contain a special character.' Fine. Then, 'Password must not contain the special character you used.' Then it asked me to include a word in Latin. Then it asked me to write a haiku about my relationship with the power grid."

According to the utility's updated security policy, passwords must now contain at least 16 characters, including uppercase and lowercase letters, two numbers, three symbols from an approved list of 11, one emoji, and no sequence of letters that "resembles a word, a name, or a human thought."

Passwords may not match any of the user's previous 400 passwords, and must be changed every 30 days.

"I don't have 400 passwords," Grant said. "I'm not a spy. I have like six, and I've been rotating them with a different number at the end since 2011. Apparently that's no longer allowed. When I tried 'Summer2026!', it said 'Too predictable.' When I tried 'MyDogIsNamedBiscuit!7', it said 'Pet names are not permitted.' I said, 'How do you know Biscuit is my pet?' It didn't answer."

Grant said her final password was accepted after 23 attempts. It reads, in full: *Qx7#vL$9!mP2@wT&🦉.*

"I'll never remember it," she said. "I wrote it on a sticky note and put it on my monitor. Which I'm pretty sure defeats the whole purpose."

The haiku portion of the requirement was added last year, according to a utility spokesperson, as part of a "creative authentication initiative."

"Studies show that personalized, emotionally meaningful input is harder for hackers to replicate," the spokesperson said. "Our system now asks users to compose a unique three-line poem each time they create a password. The haiku must follow proper 5-7-5 structure and must not rhyme, because rhyming is considered a pattern."

Grant's haiku, which was accepted on the fourth try, read:

*Power bill arrives*
*I am locked out of my life*
*Please just take my check*

Cybersecurity experts are divided on the new rules. "Strong passwords are important," said security analyst Dr. Owen Kaplan. "But at some point, the requirements become so complex that users are forced into unsafe behaviors, like writing passwords down, reusing them, or simply giving up and paying their bills by mailing cash to a PO box."

The blood sample, the utility said, is optional for customers who agree to a retina scan instead.

At press time, Grant had received an email reminding her that her new password would expire in 29 days, and that her next haiku must be "more hopeful in tone."

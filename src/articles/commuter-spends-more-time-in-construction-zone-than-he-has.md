Atlanta-area commuter Mike Reynolds, 41, calculated this week that he has spent more hours sitting in the Interstate 285 construction zone over the past three years than he has spent with his two children, a realization that has prompted him to consider moving his entire family into his car.

"I ran the numbers," Reynolds said, holding a spreadsheet on his phone. "I spend about 1 hour and 40 minutes a day sitting in the construction zone, round trip. Five days a week. Fifty weeks a year. That's 416 hours a year. Over three years, that's 1,250 hours. I spend maybe 45 minutes a day with my kids during the week, because by the time I get home, they're eating dinner, doing homework, or asleep. The construction zone is winning."

The construction project, which began in 2023 and was originally scheduled to be completed in 2025, involves widening a four-mile stretch of I-285 and rebuilding two interchanges. It is now expected to be completed in "late 2028, or thereabouts," according to the state Department of Transportation.

"I know every orange barrel by name," Reynolds said. "I know the guy who stands near Exit 31 and holds the 'Slow' sign. He's been there so long, I feel like he's family. Sometimes I wave. He waves back. I talk to him more than I talk to my brother."

Reynolds said his family has begun discussing a radical solution: moving into the car.

"If I'm going to be in the car anyway, why not bring everyone?" he said. "The kids could do their homework in the back seat. My wife could work from the passenger seat. We could eat dinner together on the shoulder. At least we'd see each other."

His wife, Jennifer, said the idea is "not as crazy as it sounds."

"We'd get more family time," she said. "Plus, the minivan has a DVD player. That's more than our living room has."

Reynolds' children were less enthusiastic. "Dad says we'd be together more," said his 9-year-old son, Tyler. "But I don't want to live on 285. I want to live in a house. With a yard. And a bathroom."

Transportation experts say Reynolds' experience is not unusual. "Long-term construction projects often impose enormous costs on commuters," said Dr. Linda Morris, a transportation economist. "Lost time, stress, fuel costs, and reduced quality of life. People don't realize how much of their lives they give up to traffic."

At press time, Reynolds had received a letter from the DOT thanking him for his "patience during this exciting period of transportation improvement," and informing him that a new phase of construction would begin next month, adding an estimated 12 minutes to his commute.

In a development historians are calling "awkward but overdue," a new national survey found that 74% of Americans now believe Las Vegas was a better value when it was run by organized crime.

"It's uncomfortable to say out loud," said Dr. Harold Weintraub, a professor of American leisure history at UNLV. "But the data is overwhelming. Under the mob, you got a $1.99 prime rib buffet beneath a crystal chandelier. Today, you get a $27 bottle of water and a sign telling you the lotion is being watched."

According to researchers, the old Vegas business model was elegant in its honesty. Rooms were cheap. Food was cheap. Drinks were free. The entire operation was built to get you onto the casino floor, where the house could take your money fair and square, or at least with a smile and a guy named Lefty wearing a pinky ring the size of a Pop-Tart.

"It was a long con, but it was a con with manners," Weintraub said. "The middle class could pretend to be rich for a weekend. That's the American dream when you strip away the bunting."

Modern Vegas, by contrast, has shifted from temptation to extraction. Resort fees, parking fees, and "amenity fees" now greet guests before they reach their rooms, which are often located at the end of hallways so labyrinthine that several resorts have begun issuing trail maps, emergency rations, and small flares.

Former casino enforcer Salvatore "Sal the Accountant" Lucchese, 91, said he doesn't recognize the town anymore.

"In my day, if you owed money, we'd have a conversation. Maybe we'd break a finger. But you knew where you stood," Lucchese said from his home in Henderson. "Now they change blackjack from 3:2 to 5:4 and nobody even tells you. That's not a game. That's an actuarial table. We had a code. These guys have a spreadsheet."

Lucchese added that he was particularly disturbed by the $25 table minimums and the loss of physical slot machines. "The old machines had levers. They clunked. When they took your money, they had the decency to make a noise about it. Now it's a touchscreen flashing like a Tokyo subway during a seizure, and you're just slapping glass while your 401(k) evaporates."

Tourism officials dismissed the survey, noting that Vegas is now a family-friendly destination offering world-class dining, entertainment, and conferences. Critics pointed out that the family-friendly Strip still features weed smoke, a man in a Pikachu costume demanding tips, and people handing out escort cards "like trading cards."

"Very Disney," Weintraub said. "Very Fantasia, if Mickey was laundering money."

The survey also found that 68% of respondents would support a return to mob rule "as long as the buffet comes back," and 22% said they would accept being buried in the desert if they could get a free drink on the way.

At press time, the city's largest resort operator had responded to the survey by introducing a $12 "Nostalgia Fee."
